Upstream Rule Violation Checklist

Appendix D

For certain rule violations, a specific process excursion or deviation could be attributed. For every rule violation that occurred, a defined set of potential causes were evaluated per the checklist below. A rule violation was stated to have no known root cause if all of the steps in the checklist resulted in no apparent indication of a causal relationship.

Step / Items Additional Directions (if applicable)
Step 1: Identify Rule Violations using JMP Control Charts. To investigate root cause of violations, refer to the following checklist: N/A
Step 2: Check the EBR to see whether the values are correct and all replicate samples are reasonably close, to make sure the average is representative. It is possible that one sample could be incorrect (i.e. one of the PCV samples came out as 100% as the equipment was not working, which incorrectly skewed the PCV final value to something unreasonable.
Step 3: Particularly for a Rule 1 violation, evaluate inoculation PCV value (the first PCV value in that stage). An atypical inoc PCV has a strong impact on SGR and can result in rule violations. For final viability violations, check the inoculation PCV of the next stage, which should be close, but somewhat lower. SGR is most affected by runtime duration and initial inoc PCV, so those are good places to start looking. A higher next stage inoculation final viability indicates the prior final viability measure was a false value.
Step 4: Evaluate the last PCV value for the prior stage, to see if poor growth in an earlier stage might be related to the cause of the violation. To understand what values are “standard,” review the product-specific ssMPS. If certain inoc PCVs are below the acceptable range of a stage that is likely the cause for a growth rate violation.
Step 5: Compare runtime durations between the run with a violation, and other recent runs. Unusually long or short run time can be the cause of a rule violation.
Step 6: Check equipment numbers and FLEX-MEIN’s to see whether the issue is occurring only with one particular piece of equipment, or if it coincides with equipment changes Some FLEX instruments may be either not working or improperly calibrated. If the same FLEX-MEIN is used exclusively for the set of runs being analyzed, it may mean the equipment is the root cause.
Step 7: If none of the above show any clues to explain the unusual value(s), begin looking through the parameters specified in the ssMPS: pH, glucose, dO2 etc. To understand what values are “standard,” review the product-specific ssMPS. Deviations from standard pH values could mean the production of too much lactate or the addition of too much base.
Step 8: Check metabolite concentrations. Large fluxes in concentrations could indicate abnormally high levels of growth. Perform statistical testing (i.e. confidence intervals, correlations, etc.) to see if certain metabolites (Na+, K+, NH4+) change at a rate that is statistically significantly different from other runs in the campaign.
Step 9: Check the subsequent stage’s corresponding KPI info. For example, if you have a rule violation and are investigating N-4 final Viability, check the trend for N-3 final viability. If the N-3 final viability data behaves (does not involve any rule 1 violations or process excursions), but does trend in the same fashion as N-4 (also above or below the centerline), the final viability violation could be linked to the culture itself.
Step 10: If those do not offer any conclusive root causes, begin looking at recent work orders on SAP and at DMS If an issue appeared abruptly after a work order, or resolved abruptly after a work order, the two may be linked.
Step 11: Evaluate raw materials. Evaluate performance associated with different lots of materials.

If these 11 steps have been evaluated and still no root cause has been identified or is discernable, the rule violation can likely be attributed to common cause variability. Control limits may need to be reevaluated.
